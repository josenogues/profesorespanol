For English speakers

Watch out for this

A guide to the trickiest gaps between English and Spanish — the points where English speakers usually get confused.

Ser vs Estar Common trap

English only has one verb "to be". Spanish has two, and picking the wrong one changes the meaning.

SER → identity, permanent traits

  • Soy americano → I am American (identity)
  • Es alto → He is tall (characteristic)
  • Son las tres → It's three o'clock

ESTAR → state, location, temporary

  • Estoy cansado → I am tired (temporary state)
  • Está en casa → He is at home (location)
  • ¿Cómo estás? → How are you? (current state)
Es listo = he's smart (trait). Está listo = he's ready (state). Same word, different verb, different meaning!

The reverse construction: Gustar

"To like" works backwards in Spanish. You don't say "I like X" — literally, "X pleases me".

EnglishSpanish (literal: "X is pleasing to me")
I like pizzaMe gusta la pizza
I like booksMe gustan los libros (plural verb because "libros" is plural)
She likes coffeeLe gusta el café
Key: the verb agrees with the thing being liked, not with the person. "Me gustan" not "yo gusto".

No subject pronoun needed

In English you must say "I speak", "she speaks" — the subject is required. In Spanish, the verb ending already tells you who's speaking, so the pronoun is usually dropped: hablo already means "I speak". Using "yo hablo" without a reason to emphasize sounds slightly unnatural, like over-explaining.

Por vs Para Common trap

English uses "for" for both — Spanish splits it into two words with different logic.

POR → cause, means, duration, exchange

  • Gracias por la ayuda → Thanks for the help (cause)
  • Voy por la calle → I walk along the street (route/means)
  • Trabajo por tres horas → I work for three hours (duration)
  • Lo cambié por otro → I exchanged it for another one

PARA → purpose, recipient, deadline

  • Estudio para aprobar → I study (in order) to pass (purpose)
  • Este regalo es para ti → This gift is for you (recipient)
  • Para mañana, listo → Ready by tomorrow (deadline)
  • Trabajo para una empresa → I work for a company (employer)
Compare: Es para ti = this is for you, meant for you (recipient). Es por ti = this is because of you (cause/reason). Same "for" in English, opposite logic in Spanish.

Adjectives usually go after the noun

Unlike English, most Spanish adjectives follow the noun: la casa blanca (the white house), not "la blanca casa". A handful of common adjectives can go before the noun for emphasis or with a slightly different nuance (un gran hombre vs un hombre grande), but that's the exception, not the rule.
