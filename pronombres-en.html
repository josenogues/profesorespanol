Pronombres

Pronouns replace a noun. Spanish has several types, and object pronouns (direct/indirect) are usually the trickiest because they sometimes attach to the verb.

In Spanish, the subject pronoun (yo, tú, él…) is almost always dropped, because the verb ending already tells you who's speaking: hablo already means "I speak". Unlike English, you don't need "yo hablo" unless you want to add emphasis or avoid ambiguity.

Where they go

Before the conjugated verb

Lo veo. / No te entiendo.

The most common position.

Attached to infinitive, gerund, and affirmative commands

Quiero verlo. / Estoy haciéndolo. / ¡Dímelo!

Written as one single word, sometimes needing a written accent.

Two pronouns together: indirect + direct

Se lo di. (le + lo → se lo)

When "le/les" comes right before "lo/la/los/las", it becomes "se".

Possessives and demonstratives

Possessives

mi/mis, tu/tus, su/sus, nuestro/a/os/as, vuestro/a/os/as, su/sus

They agree in number (and sometimes gender) with the thing owned, not with the owner: nuestra casa, nuestros libros. And unlike some languages, no article goes in front: mi libro, never "the my book".

Demonstratives

este/esta (near me), ese/esa (near you), aquel/aquella (far from both of us)

English only has "this/that", but Spanish has a three-way distance system — "ese" (near the listener) vs "aquel" (far from everyone) is the distinction that doesn't map onto English.
