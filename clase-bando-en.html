Every noun picks a team

Gender, agreement, and why the adjective goes last

1. The 90% rule

Ends in -o → el. Ends in -a → la. El libro, la mesa. That covers most of the language.

The other endings worth learning

Always la

  • -ción → la canción
  • -sión → la televisión
  • -dad → la ciudad
  • -tad → la libertad

Usually el

  • -or → el color
  • -aje → el viaje
  • -e → could be either: el coche, la noche

2. The exceptions that actually come up

Not a dictionary — just the ones you'll say this month.

End in -a, take el

  • el problema
  • el tema
  • el día
  • el mapa
  • el idioma
  • el clima

End in -o, take la

  • la mano
  • la foto
  • la moto
  • la radio
El agua is feminine. It takes el only because saying «la agua» is a mouthful — and the adjective gives it away: el agua fría, not el agua frío.

3. The adjective copies the noun — and goes behind it

Una casa roja, never una roja casa. Whatever the noun ends in, the adjective ends in too.

oneseveral
masculineel coche rojolos coches rojos
femininela casa rojalas casas rojas
Half of them don't even change

Adjectives ending in -e or in a consonant are the same for both: grande, verde, azul, fácil, difícil. Un coche verde, una casa verde. Only the plural moves.

4. The plural, in one line

Ends in a vowel → add -s. Ends in a consonant → add -es.

casa → casascoche → coches
color → coloresciudad → ciudades
The one that gives you away: Mi padre es una buena persona. The word persona is feminine whoever you're talking about. The gender belongs to the word, not to the person — la mesa isn't female either.
