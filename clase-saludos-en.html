The first two minutes

Saying hello, saying who you are and getting out again. Short words, and three traps that English does not warn you about.

1. Hello, by the clock

Spanish splits the day in three, and the splits are not where you would put them.

whennote
Holaany time at allnever wrong, never formal
Buenos díasuntil about 2pmuntil lunch, not until noon
Buenas tardesfrom lunch until darkthat is 2pm to 9pm in summer
Buenas nochesonce it is darkhello and goodbye
Buenas noches does both jobs. English has «good evening» to arrive and «good night» to leave. Spanish uses the same words for both, so you will hear it walking into a restaurant at ten and again on the way out at midnight.

2. Who is who

Before the next bit you need the people. English has three words here; Spanish has eight, and two of them mean «you».

one personmore than one
meyo Inosotros · nosotras we
youtú informal
usted formal		vosotros · vosotras informal, Spain
ustedes formal — and everybody in Latin America
themél · ella he, sheellos · ellas they
And then you leave them out. The verb ending already says who, so hablo español is the normal sentence and yo hablo español sounds like you are correcting somebody. Keep the pronoun only when you really are: yo voy, tú te quedas. The -as and -os forms are for a group of women and for everyone else.

3. tú or usted — the one English does not have

Two words for «you». You have to pick one every time you speak, and the verb changes with it.

túusted
whoalmost everybodymuch older, or very formal
how are you?¿Cómo estás?¿Cómo está?
what’s your name?¿Cómo te llamas?¿Cómo se llama?
where are you from?¿De dónde eres?¿De dónde es?
In Spain, default to tú. It is what you will hear in shops, in bars and at work, even with people older than you. Usted is for a very formal setting or someone much older — and if in doubt, they will tell you: puedes tutearme, «you can use tú with me». In Latin America the balance is different, but that is another class.

4. How are you, and what to answer

Unlike English, this is a real question with a short real answer. Nobody expects your life story, but they do expect more than nothing.

questionanswer
¿Qué tal? (the everyday one)Bien, ¿y tú?
¿Cómo estás?Muy bien, gracias.
¿Cómo va todo?Bastante bien.
— and if it is not greatRegular. · Vamos tirando.
Always bounce it back with ¿y tú? Answering just bien and stopping sounds like you want the conversation to end. Two extra letters fix it.

5. Saying who you are

Two ways to give your name, and one word that trips up every English speaker.

SpanishEnglish
Me llamo Edward.My name is Edward.
Soy Edward.I’m Edward.
Encantado / Encantada.Nice to meet you.
Mucho gusto.Pleased to meet you.
Igualmente.Likewise. (the reply)
Soy de Londres.I’m from London.
Encantado changes with you, not with them. A man says encantado and a woman says encantada, whoever they are talking to. You are saying «I am delighted», so the word describes you.

6. Getting out

This is the trap that makes beginners sound like they are leaving forever.

Spanishwhen
Hasta luegothe normal one — use this
Hasta mañanasee you tomorrow
Nos vemossee you around
Chaocasual, with friends
Adiósfinal, or just formal
Adiós is not the everyday goodbye. English textbooks teach it first, but in a Spanish shop you will hear hasta luego twenty times for every adiós — even to someone you will never see again. Adiós carries a hint of «for good».

7. And before you say anything

Two gestures that come with the words, so they do not catch you out.

Two kisses

  • Between women, and between a woman and a man.
  • Right cheek first, then left.
  • Friends, family, and often a first introduction in a social setting.

A handshake

  • Between two men.
  • At work, with anyone, the first time.
  • Always fine. Nobody is ever offended by a hand.
If you are not sure, put your hand out. It works everywhere and nobody reads anything into it. The kisses will happen to you soon enough.
