Género y número

Every Spanish noun has gender (masculine or feminine) and number (singular or plural). It's not always intuitive, so it helps to learn the patterns and the most common exceptions.

Forming the feminine

RuleMasculineFeminine
-o → -achicochica
Consonant + aprofesorprofesora
-e and some consonants: unchangedestudianteestudiante

Forming the plural

Ends in...RuleExample

Exceptions that catch people out

Masculine words ending in -a

el problema, el día, el mapa, el idioma, el planeta

They come from Greek; they behave as masculine even though they end in -a.

Feminine words that use "el"

el agua, el águila, el hacha

These are feminine, but use "el" in the singular because they start with a stressed "a" sound. Plural: las aguas.

Meaning changes with gender

el capital (money/capital) / la capital (capital city)

el orden (sequence/order) / la orden (a command, or a religious order)

Trick: whenever you're unsure, learn the noun together with its article ("la mano", not just "mano") — that's the most reliable way to memorize gender correctly. Since English has no grammatical gender at all, there's no shortcut from your own language here — it's mostly repetition.
