Three verbs that carry another one

Soler, ir a and tener que. Learn these three and you can talk about what you usually do, what you are going to do and what you have to do — without a single new tense.

1. The shape is always the same

One verb you conjugate, one verb you leave alone. The second one never changes: it stays in the infinitive, ending in -ar, -er or -ir.

verb 1 (changes)verb 2 (never changes)meaning
habitsuelocomerI usually eat
planvoy acomerI’m going to eat
obligationtengo quecomerI have to eat
You already know this shape. It is exactly how English works too: «I have to eat», «I’m going to eat». Only the first part moves.

2. soler — the one English does not have

English says «I usually…» with an adverb. Spanish has a whole verb for it, and using it is the fastest way to stop sounding like a textbook.

solerexample
yosueloSuelo levantarme a las siete.
túsueles¿Sueles comer aquí?
él / ellasueleSuele llegar tarde.
nosotrossolemosSolemos cenar a las nueve.
vosotrossoléis¿Soléis venir los lunes?
ellossuelenSuelen trabajar hasta tarde.
It is an o → ue verb, with the boot shape from the present class. And it has no imperative and no future: you cannot order someone to usually do something.

3. tener que, and the one that has no owner

For something you have to do, tener que. For a rule that applies to everybody, hay que — and then there is no person at all.

SpanishEnglishwho
Tengo que estudiar.I have to study.me
Tienes que venir.You have to come.you
Hay que estudiar mucho.One has to study a lot.everyone / no one
Hay que pagar antes.You have to pay first.it is the rule
Hay que never changes. Not for person, not for number. There is no hayo que. If you want to say who, you need tener que.
