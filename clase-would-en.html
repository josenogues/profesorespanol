Would, could, should

Three little English words that have no Spanish equivalent at all. Spanish changes the end of the verb instead — and one of the three is a trap, because «would» is sometimes not this tense.

1. The easiest tense you will learn, again

You already built the future this way: you keep the whole infinitive and add the ending. The conditional does exactly the same, with a different set.

hablarcomervivir
yohablaríacomeríaviviría
túhablaríascomeríasvivirías
él / ella / ustedhablaríacomeríaviviría
nosotroshablaríamoscomeríamosviviríamos
vosotroshablaríaiscomeríaisviviríais
ellos / ustedeshablaríancomeríanvivirían
All six carry a tilde, and the irregulars are the same twelve as the future. tendría, pondría, saldría, vendría, haría, diría, podría, sabría, querría, habría. If you learned them for the future, you already know them here.

2. could — and the trap underneath it

English «could» does two different jobs and Spanish splits them. Get this one wrong and you are saying the opposite of what you mean.

EnglishSpanishwhat it really says
I could help you (if you want)Podría ayudarte.hypothetical, offer
I could swim at five (as a child)Podía nadar a los cinco.I was able to, repeatedly
I could finally open itPude abrirlo.I managed to, once
Could you pass me the salt?¿Podrías pasarme la sal?the polite one
Podría is not the past of puedo. It is the «if» version. For «I could» meaning «I was able to», you need podía or pude — the past tenses. English piles all three into one word and leaves you to sort it out.

3. should, and would like

The other two are kinder: one verb each, and you already half know them.

EnglishSpanish
I should studyDebería estudiar.from deber
You should goDeberías ir.
We ought to call herTendríamos que llamarla.softer than tenemos que
I would like a coffeeMe gustaría un café.the polite me gusta
I would rather stayPreferiría quedarme.
Querría un café and me gustaría un café are both fine — and both are much softer than quiero un café, which to a Spanish ear is an order, not a request. This is the first half of the «sounding polite» class.

4. The «would» that is not this tense at all

This is the one nobody warns you about. In English, «would» also means «used to» — and there the Spanish is the imperfect, not the conditional.

Englishwrongright
When I was a kid I would go to my grandma’siría a casa de mi abuelaiba a casa de mi abuela
Every summer we would swim in the rivernadaríamos en el ríonadábamos en el río
He would always arrive latellegaría siempre tardellegaba siempre tarde
Test it in English: can you swap «would» for «used to»? If yes, it is a past habit and you want the imperfect. If you can swap it for «if things were different», it is the conditional. Iría means «I would go if…», which is a different sentence entirely.

5. And one more use: the polite past

Spanish softens by moving the verb away from the present. The conditional is one step; the imperfect is another.

bluntsoftersoftest
Quiero hablar contigo.Querría hablar contigo.Quería hablar contigo.
Puedes ayudarme.¿Podrías ayudarme?—
Tienes que venir.Tendrías que venir.—
Yes, quería — the past — to ask for something now. Hola, quería un café is what you say walking into a bar. It is not a mistake and it is not the past: it is distance, and distance is politeness.
