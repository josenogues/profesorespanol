Pronunciation and phonetics

Spanish sounds, from the basics to the special cases: vowels, diphthongs, how to divide into syllables, accent marks, and letters with their own rules. Press to hear any example.

Sounds

The vowels

Spanish has only 5 vowel sounds, and each one always sounds the same — unlike English, where the same letter can sound completely different depending on the word (think of the "a" in "cat" vs "father" vs "about"). Learn these 5 sounds well and there won't be any surprises.

VowelExample
acasa
emesa
ilibro
osol
uluna
Watch out: Spanish doesn't have the vowel reduction English has (that "uh" sound in unstressed syllables, like the "a" in "about"). Every Spanish vowel keeps its one, single sound, always.
Sounds

Diphthongs, triphthongs and hiatus

When two or more vowels sit next to each other, sometimes they're pronounced as one syllable (diphthong/triphthong), and sometimes they split into separate syllables (hiatus). Knowing which is which helps you divide words correctly and place accent marks in the right spot.

Diphthong

  • A strong vowel (a, e, o) + a weak one (i, u), or two weak ones together
  • Pronounced as one single syllable
  • bien, cielo, cuatro, ciudad

Triphthong

  • Weak + strong + weak, all three together
  • Not very common, but it exists
  • buey, cambiáis, limpiáis

Hiatus

  • Two strong vowels together, or a weak one carrying an accent mark
  • Split into separate syllables
  • le-o, pa-ís, dí-a
Trick: if a weak vowel (i, u) carries a written accent next to a strong one, it's always a hiatus, not a diphthong — that's why "país" splits into pa-ís, two syllables, instead of being pronounced as one.
Spelling

Syllable division

Knowing how to split a word into syllables is the foundation for everything else: it tells you where the accent mark goes, and helps you pronounce words correctly. The general rule is simple.

RuleExample
A single consonant between vowels joins the next vowelca-sa, pe-ro
CH, LL and RR never split (they count as one letter)mu-cha-cho, ca-lle, pe-rro
Two consonants together: usually split apartcua-der-no (cua is a diphthong), ac-ción
Groups with L or R (pl, pr, bl, br, cl, cr...) stay togetherha-blar, li-bro, pla-ya
Remember: a diphthong counts as one syllable (bien = 1 syllable), but a hiatus counts as two (le-o = 2 syllables). So figure out whether it's a diphthong or a hiatus before dividing into syllables.
Spelling

Tildes (written accents)

Unlike English (which barely uses written accents at all), Spanish accent marks follow fixed, predictable rules — they're not random or decorative.

Llanas (stress on 2nd-to-last syllable)

  • The most common stress pattern in Spanish
  • Gets a written accent ONLY if it does NOT end in -n, -s, or a vowel
  • árbol, fácil, azúcar

Agudas (stress on last syllable)

  • Gets a written accent ONLY if it DOES end in -n, -s, or a vowel
  • hablé, jardín, autobús

Esdrújulas (stress further back)

  • Stress on the 3rd-to-last syllable or earlier
  • ALWAYS gets a written accent, no exceptions
  • rápido, música, dígamelo

Spanish's special letters and sounds

Spanish has sounds and letters that don't exist in English. Here are the essential rules!

Double letters

  • LL → a soft "y" sound: llamar, llave, pollo
  • RR → a strong rolled r: perro, carro, correr
  • CC → sounds like "ks": acceso, acción, accidente

H and CH

  • H → always silent: hola, hombre, haber
  • CH → like English "ch" in "chip": noche, coche, chico, mucho

GUE / GUI / GÜE / GÜI

  • GUE, GUI → the U is silent: guerra, guiar (sounds like "geh", "ghee")
  • GÜE, GÜI → the Ü IS pronounced (with a dieresis): pingüino

The Ñ

  • A letter unique to Spanish, sounds like "ny" in "canyon".
  • Examples: España, año, mañana, señor, niño

The Q

  • QUE, QUI → the U is always silent: que, quiero, queso, quince (reads as "keh", "kee")
  • QUA or QUO don't exist in Spanish: it's written cuatro, cuando

The X

  • Usually sounds like "ks": éxito, taxi, sexo
  • In some words of indigenous origin it sounds like "sh": México, Oaxaca, Texas
