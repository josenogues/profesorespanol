Fonética

Spanish pronunciation and spelling — the letters that behave differently from English, and the rules for written accents.

Spelling

Spanish's special letters and sounds

Spanish has sounds and letters that don't exist in English. Here are the essential rules!

Double letters

  • LL → a soft "y" sound: llamar, llave, pollo
  • RR → a strong rolled r: perro, carro, correr
  • CC → sounds like "ks": acceso, acción, accidente

H and CH

  • H → always silent: hola, hombre, haber
  • CH → like English "ch" in "chip": noche, coche, chico, mucho

GUE / GUI / GÜE / GÜI

  • GUE, GUI → the U is silent: guerra, guiar (sounds like "geh", "ghee")
  • GÜE, GÜI → the Ü IS pronounced (with a dieresis): pingüino

The Ñ

  • A letter unique to Spanish, sounds like "ny" in "canyon".
  • Examples: España, año, mañana, señor, niño

The Q

  • QUE, QUI → the U is always silent: que, quiero, queso, quince (reads as "keh", "kee")
  • QUA or QUO don't exist in Spanish: it's written cuatro, cuando

The X

  • Usually sounds like "ks": éxito, taxi, sexo
  • In some words of indigenous origin it sounds like "sh": México, Oaxaca, Texas

Tildes (written accents)

Unlike English (which barely uses written accents at all), Spanish accent marks follow fixed, predictable rules — they're not random or decorative.

Llanas (stress on 2nd-to-last syllable)

  • The most common stress pattern in Spanish
  • Gets a written accent ONLY if it does NOT end in -n, -s, or a vowel
  • árbol, fácil, azúcar

Agudas (stress on last syllable)

  • Gets a written accent ONLY if it DOES end in -n, -s, or a vowel
  • hablé, jardín, autobús

Esdrújulas (stress further back)

  • Stress on the 3rd-to-last syllable or earlier
  • ALWAYS gets a written accent, no exceptions
  • rápido, música, dígamelo
Common trap: sometimes the accent mark changes the word's meaning entirely: tú (you) vs tu (your); él (he) vs el (the); sí (yes) vs si (if). Here the accent isn't about pronunciation — it distinguishes two different words.
