Si tuviera dinero…

Imagining things: one fixed formula and one new form you almost know already

1. The formula, and what changes

A sentence with si can talk about something that may happen or something you are imagining. What changes is the two verbs, always as a pair — just like if I have money and if I had money.

the «si» halfthe other half
pretérito imperfecto de subjuntivo
(imperfect subjunctive)		condicional simple
(conditional)
si tuviera dinerome compraría una casa

And next to the real-world version, to see what changes:

the sentencewhat it saysthe tenses
may happenSi tengo dinero, me compro una casa.you have money, or you mightpresent + present
you’re imagining itSi tuviera dinero, me compraría una casa.you don’t have money: it’s a hypothesisimperfect subjunctive + conditional
the other way roundMe compraría una casa si tuviera dinero.the order of the halves doesn’t matterconditional + imperfect subjunctive
Two halves, two tenses, always the same ones. The si half takes the pretérito imperfecto de subjuntivo (tuviera = I had); the other half takes the condicional simple (compraría = I would buy). Those are the names you will find in any conjugation table. They never swap.

2. The pretérito imperfecto de subjuntivo comes from a form you already know

Take the they form of the preterite —ellos tuvieron—, drop the -ron and add -ra. That’s it. If the preterite is irregular, this form inherits it without asking.

Every irregular preterite gives you this tense for free. The ones you learned for the past —tuve, hice, dije, pude, fui— are the same ones. · Watch nosotros: it takes an accent, tuviéramos, fuéramos. · And ser and ir share the form: si fuera rico, si fuera a Madrid.

3. The mistake you will make: «si tendría»

After si, when you are imagining, Spanish never uses the conditional. You will be tempted, because English sometimes says if I would.

like thisnot like this
Si tuviera tiempo, iría.Si tendría tiempo, iría.
Si pudiera, te ayudaría.Si podría, te ayudaría.
Si fuera tú, no lo haría.Si sería tú, no lo haría.
You will hear it, and that doesn’t make it right. In the north of Spain —the Basque Country, Navarre, La Rioja— people often say si tendría, but the standard is si tuviera, and it’s what’s expected in any exam or work email.

4. The same form, two more places

The same pretérito imperfecto de subjuntivo —tuviera— also covers two things people say every day.

sentencewhat it says
Ojalá tuviera más tiempo.I wish I had more time
Ojalá estuvieras aquí.I wish you were here
Habla como si fuera el jefe.He talks as if he were the boss
Me miró como si no me conociera.She looked at me as if she didn’t know me
Ojalá with the present and with this form are not the same. Ojalá tenga tiempo: I hope I’ll have time. Ojalá tuviera tiempo: I wish I had — and I don’t. The form tells you whether it’s still possible.

5. One step further: what didn’t happen

For a hypothesis about the past, each half moves to its compound tense: the imperfect subjunctive becomes the pluscuamperfecto de subjuntivo, and the condicional simple becomes the condicional compuesto — just like if I had had, I would have bought.

pretérito pluscuamperfecto de subjuntivo
(pluperfect subjunctive)

pretérito pluscuamperfecto de subjuntivo
(pluperfect subjunctive)		condicional simple
(conditional)		the past changes the present

And in sentences:

sentencewhat it saysthe tenses
Si hubiera tenido dinero, habría comprado la casa.If I’d had money, I’d have bought the housepluperfect subj. + conditional perfect
Si me lo hubieras dicho, habría venido.If you’d told me, I’d have comepluperfect subj. + conditional perfect
Si hubiera estudiado, ahora tendría un trabajo mejor.If I’d studied, I’d have a better job nowpluperfect subj. + conditional
Hubiera is just haber in the new form, from hubieron. The participle never changes. · And you can mix them: the third sentence puts the past in one half and the present in the other, exactly as English does.
