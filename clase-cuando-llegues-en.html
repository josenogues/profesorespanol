Cuando llegues

When Spanish asks for the subjunctive — and the two places everyone gets it wrong

1. The form: from «yo», with the vowel swapped

Take the yo form of the present, drop the -o and put the opposite vowel: -ar verbs go to e; -er and -ir verbs go to a.

infinitiveyo · presentsubjunctive
hablarhablohable
comercomocoma
vivirvivoviva
tenertengotenga
hacerhagohaga
conocerconozcoconozca
volvervuelvovuelva

And six that don’t come from yo and you just learn:

ser → seair → vaya
estar → estésaber → sepa
haber → hayadar → dé
If the yo is odd, so is the subjunctive. Tengo → tenga, hago → haga, conozco → conozca, vuelvo → vuelva. That’s why the present yo is worth knowing cold: it gives you this whole tense.

2. When it’s needed: three families

English has almost nothing like this, so here is the whole idea: the subjunctive goes in the second half, after que, when the first half isn’t stating a fact but wanting, feeling or doubting.

the first half…examples
wants or asks for somethingQuiero que vengas.
Te pido que me ayudes.
feels or judges somethingMe alegra que estés aquí.
Es importante que llegues a tiempo.
doubts or denies somethingNo creo que sea verdad.
Dudo que venga.
Only when the person changes. Quiero que vengas: I want, you come. If it’s the same person, use the infinitive: quiero ir (I want to go), never quiero que yo vaya.

3. «Cuando» about the future

This is where English speakers trip. English says when you arrive in the present and feels safe; Spanish wants to know whether it has happened yet.

like thisnot like this
hasn’t happened yetCuando llegues, llámame.
Cuando sea mayor, viviré en el campo.		Cuando llegarás
Cuando llegas, llámame
always happensCuando llego a casa, ceno.
already happenedCuando llegué, ya no había nadie.

En cuanto (as soon as), hasta que (until) and después de que (after) work the same way: about the future, subjunctive. And antes de que (before) always takes it, because what comes after it hasn’t happened yet by definition.

Never the future after cuando. The future goes in the other half: cuando llegues, te llamaré — when you arrive, I’ll call you.

4. «Creo que» takes the normal tense

Creer and pensar state what you hold to be true, so they take the indicative. As soon as you deny them, they turn into doubt and want the subjunctive.

like thisnot like thiswhy
Creo que es verdad.Creo que sea verdad.you take it as true
No creo que sea verdad.No creo que es verdad.you deny it: now it’s doubt
Pienso que viene.Pienso que venga.you take it as true
Me parece que tienes razón.you take it as true
It looks backwards, but it isn’t. I think sounds unsure in English; in Spanish creo que is an opinion you’re asserting. The doubt only comes with no.
